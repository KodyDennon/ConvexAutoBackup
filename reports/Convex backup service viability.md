# Sell hosted Convex backups to agencies

As of 8 October 2026, the version of ConvexAutoBackup a founder can sell is a hosted control plane on a VPS they administer, with archives in Cloudflare R2, priced as an agency console at about **$99 per month**. The buyer who will actually pay is an agency or freelancer who already operates many client Convex projects and wants one login, retention past Convex’s own window, and a copy that lives outside Convex. A venture-scale company whose pitch is “Convex does not back you up” is a bad idea: Convex Professional already includes daily logical snapshots, generic backup hosts cannot speak Convex’s export format, and the only public Convex recovery request is eight people asking Convex itself for point-in-time recovery. The realistic company keeps the open-source self-hosted app, sells operator custody of deploy keys and archives with an honest security page, and also writes archives into the customer’s own R2 bucket. Operator storage on the planning scenarios is about **$0.04 to $4.26 per deployment per month**. The customer’s Convex read is the bill that hurts. Launch work is shared Postgres tenancy, real sessions and MFA, and envelope encryption whose wrapping key stays on the VPS. SOC 2 is a later examination for buyers who procure that way, after those controls have run, and it is unnecessary for the first design partners.

## Agencies with a book of Convex clients are who pays

The payer is the person who already feels the pain of many deployments and one tired operator. SimpleBackups prices that console at **$99 per month for 20 backup jobs**, with projects, access control, and a disaster-recovery dashboard, and labels the plan for SaaS, agencies, and SMEs ([SimpleBackups pricing](https://simplebackups.com/pricing)). SnapShooter’s Agency plan is **$199 per month for up to 250 resources**, with unlimited bring-your-own storage ([SnapShooter pricing](https://snapshooter.com/pricing)). DailyBkup’s agency license is **$49 per month** for a multi-client workspace whose storage is the customer’s own cloud ([DailyBkup pricing](https://dailybkup.com/pricing)). Those three public packages, **$49, $99, and $199**, are the market the hosted product enters. WordPress care plans show the same commercial shape from the other side of the invoice: agencies resell “backups included” inside a retainer, and one vendor claims its customers sell care at **$99 to $499 per client per month** while the underlying work costs less ([White Label WP care](https://whitelabelwp.agency/care/)). That range is a vendor’s claim about its own customers. The pattern is still the right one for Convex. The agency pays for the console. The client often never sees a backup line item.

Solo developers are the weakest incremental buyers, and Free and Starter teams are weaker still. Convex Professional is **$25 per developer per month**, capped at 1–20 developers, and the pricing page lists daily backups as included ([Convex pricing](https://www.convex.dev/pricing)). SnapShooter Lite is **$14 per month** for up to three resources. SimpleBackups gives the first job away. Supabase tells free projects to dump with the CLI and keep their own off-site copy, and a Supabase-specific tool markets itself as free for one project ([Supabase backups](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/backups), [VaultKeep](https://vaultkeep.dev/blog/supabase-free-tier-backups-github-actions/)). A Convex developer who will run a binary already has ConvexAutoBackup. The public crate `convex-autobackup-core` showed **232 downloads** at `0.1.0-beta.6` in this research, which is a small installed base, and the hosted offer has to be the thing that binary does not do: operate the schedule for a customer who will not host it ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/convex-autobackup-core)).

The buyer to approach later, after the agency console exists, is a Professional team that wants history longer than Convex keeps and a copy Convex cannot delete with the account. The buyer to leave until the controls are real is the company already on Convex Business. That plan is a **$2,500 monthly minimum**, and active dedicated deployments were **$2,800 per month for D1024** and **$5,600 per month for D2048** in US East on the enterprise PDF current as of 28 September 2026 ([Convex enterprise pricing PDF](https://www.convex.dev/enterprise-pricing.pdf)). Those hosts already include physical backups the customer cannot download, restored only through Convex support, and kept up to seven days ([Backup & Restore](https://docs.convex.dev/database/backup-restore)). A team that buys that way will ask for the backup vendor’s own SOC 2, a BAA, and a questionnaire. Convex’s August 2026 funding posts are a poor sizing tool for any of these buyers: the company claimed close to **500,000 developers**, nearly **2 million applications**, and **1.2 million weekly npm downloads**, and said dozens of companies were on Enterprise plans ([PR Newswire, 4 August 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/convex-raises-57m-series-b-led-by-insight-partners-to-scale-the-reliable-backend-for-the-ai-era-302841686.html), [Convex Series B post](https://news.convex.dev/convex-raises-57m/)). On 8 October 2026 the pricing page badge read **“npm 2M weekly.”** None of those figures is a count of teams that would pay a third party. No public count of Professional subscribers or Convex agencies was found.

Specificity is the product and the ceiling. SimpleBackups’ database connectors are MySQL, PostgreSQL, MongoDB, and Redis. SnapShooter’s catalog is servers, snapshots, and those engines. Convex’s archive is a ZIP of per-table `documents.jsonl` plus an optional `_storage` folder, produced by the dashboard or `npx convex export` ([Backup & Restore](https://docs.convex.dev/database/backup-restore)). A Convex customer cannot buy those hosts as a drop-in. The companies that started as narrow backup products grew by leaving the niche: Rewind still sells Shopify backup from **$19 per month** and now prices Jira, GitHub, and monday.com per user ([Shopify app listing](https://apps.shopify.com/backup), [Rewind pricing](https://rewind.com/pricing/)). Expecting Rewind-shaped revenue from Convex alone fights the only Convex-specific demand signal on the record. Ship request #363, “Point in Time Recovery (PITR, Backups by Second),” was submitted on 11 June 2026, remained **Requested** with no development updates, and showed **85 backing points from 8 supporters** ([Ship request #363](https://ship.convex.dev/requests/js7dggash7ddpw301ckdhaggp188fbkr)). Those eight people asked Convex, and they asked for restore-to-the-second. A scheduled full ZIP answers a different question.

## The product sells the copy Convex will not keep

Convex Cloud already ships logical snapshot backup and restore. A backup is a consistent snapshot of table data, optionally including file storage, and it omits deployment code, environment variables, and pending scheduled functions ([Backup & Restore](https://docs.convex.dev/database/backup-restore)). Manual snapshots are stored for **7 days**. Daily periodic backups are stored for **7 days** and weekly backups for **14 days**. Periodic backups require Professional. Free and Starter deployments can keep **at most two** backups at a time. Professional can keep many, billed as usage. The pricing comparison row “Daily backups” is No on Free and Starter and Yes on Professional and above ([Convex pricing](https://www.convex.dev/pricing)). Generating a backup reads the database and, when files are included, the file store; the backup object itself is billed like user file storage. Dedicated D1024 and D2048 classes add automatic physical backups every 24 hours, kept up to 7 days, included in the hardware price, **not downloadable**, and restorable only by contacting Convex support. A third-party service cannot call that physical path. Its Cloud interface is the logical ZIP.

The gap worth money is therefore narrow and real. It is retention past 7 or 14 days, a copy that survives a Convex account or region failure, a cadence the dashboard checkbox does not offer, an archive in a bucket the customer controls, and evidence that a restore was drilled. It sits beside a first-party backup the customer can already turn on. Point-in-time recovery is absent from the Backup & Restore page fetched on 8 October 2026. Supabase, for comparison, includes 7-day daily backups on Pro (from **$25 per month**) and sells 7-day point-in-time recovery at about **$100 per month per project**, with 14 and 28 days at about **$200 and $400** ([Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing), [Supabase PITR usage](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/manage-your-usage/point-in-time-recovery)). Convex has no equivalent add-on. A hosted daily ZIP does not become one by being off-platform. Selling “PITR for Convex” would be selling a feature the export API does not provide.

The credential the hosted service should accept is a permission-scoped deploy key, and the security page has to say what that key can do. Deploy keys are service tokens. Once issued, they keep their original permissions even if the creator is later demoted; revocation is deleting the key ([Role actions](https://docs.convex.dev/team-management/role-actions)). The actions that match a backup vendor are `deployment:backups:create` and `deployment:backups:download`. File inclusion is a flag on the export, and the docs do not list a separate file-storage permission. That key can read every table document and, with the flag, every stored file. It is the wrong product if the customer pastes a production deploy key minted only for `deployment:deploy`, and it is a worse product if they paste an admin key. Admin keys are described as an irrevocable secret baked into the deployment, usable without a call to convex.dev ([Deploy keys](https://docs.convex.dev/cli/deploy-key-types)). `deployment:backups:import` is a different blast radius: import with `--replace` wipes the deployment’s existing data ([Data import](https://docs.convex.dev/database/import-export/import)). One-click restore into production is a second product on top of export. The open-source export worker allows only one requested or in-progress export at a time ([export worker](https://github.com/get-convex/convex-backend/blob/88a2a911/crates/application/src/exports/worker.rs)). If Convex Cloud uses that worker, the hosted scheduler has to serialize exports per deployment, including against Convex’s own periodic backup. Docs do not publish a maximum ZIP size, a timeout, or a daily quota. The honest operations page says duration is “seconds to hours” and that overlap behavior is unconfirmed in Cloud’s SLA.

## A 20 GB daily export costs the customer about $70

Convex does not publish a distribution of deployment sizes. The planning sizes used here, **200 MB, 2 GB, and 20 GB**, sit inside documented bounds: Professional includes **50 GB** of database storage and **100 GB** of file storage, serverless classes cap the dataset at **1 TB**, and a 20 GB export is a large file store that is still a normal Convex app ([Convex limits](https://docs.convex.dev/production/state/limits)). They are scenarios, not measurements. The only public export anecdote with a size is Convex 1.9’s note that one image over **500 MB** works, alongside a table of **10 million documents** with no byte total and no elapsed time ([Announcing Convex 1.9](https://news.convex.dev/announcing-convex-1-9/)).

Operator cost below is Cloudflare R2 Standard, list price, no account free tier, one destination, a 5 percent full-upload retry, and one restore download per deployment per month. R2’s pricing page, last updated **1 October 2026**, is **$0.015 per GB-month**, Class A operations **$4.50 per million**, Class B **$0.36 per million**, and direct internet egress **$0** when bytes leave through the S3 API, the Workers API, or an `r2.dev` domain ([Cloudflare R2 pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)). Infrequent Access is the wrong class for 14-day retention: deleted objects are still billed for **30 days**, and reads cost **$0.01 per GB**. Request charges in these scenarios are cents. Age-encryption overhead is treated as negligible. Worker CPU, the VPS, payment fees, and support are not in the storage column. Convex database I/O and file egress are billed on the customer’s deployment, because the export uses the customer’s deploy key.

| Scenario | Copies kept | R2 storage and requests | Professional file egress if the 50 GB allowance is already used | Professional file egress if that allowance is still free |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Solo, 0.2 GB, daily, keep 14 | 14 | $0.04 | $0.72 | $0 |
| Typical, 2 GB, 30 daily plus 8 weekly | 38 | $1.15 | $8.16 | $2.16 |
| File-heavy, 20 GB, daily, keep 14 | 14 | $4.26 | $72.00 | $66.00 |
| Agency book, 15 solo and 10 typical | mixed | $12.12 | about $92 across the book | about $22 across the book |

The 70 percent storage-margin floor on R2, price at least equal to cost divided by 0.30, is about **$0.15, $3.83, $14.20, and $1.62** average for those four rows. A flat **$9 per deployment** clears that bar on the solo and typical rows (about 99 percent and 87 percent) and fails the 20 GB daily row (about **53 percent**). **$15 per deployment** is the first round price in the model that clears 70 percent for a 20 GB daily archive kept 14 copies on R2 (about **72 percent**). A flat **$99 agency workspace** on the modeled 25-deployment mix has storage cost **$12.12**, about **88 percent** storage margin. Twenty typical deployments at $1.15 each are **$23** of R2, still about **77 percent**. Twenty file-heavy daily deployments are about **$85** of R2 inside a $99 plan, about **14 percent**. The agency row’s Convex figures are the sum of each client’s own bill, each client burning that client’s Professional allowance. The $99 plan therefore needs a retained-size cap or an overage. Customer-owned R2 is how an agency keeps the large clients inside the console price: operator storage for those jobs goes to zero, and the customer’s bucket bill is theirs.

The customer constraint binds before the operator’s margin does. A 20 GB daily export reads **600 GB a month**. On Professional in the US, file egress is **$0.12 per GB** after **50 GB** included, so the overage is about **$66** if the allowance is otherwise unused and **$72** if the app has already used it ([Convex limits](https://docs.convex.dev/production/state/limits)). Database I/O, if the ZIP is documents, is **$0.20 per GB** and lands around **$110 to $120**. The operator’s R2 bill for 14 copies of that archive is **$4.26**. Raising the hosted price does not fix a purchase that costs the customer an order of magnitude more at Convex than at the backup vendor. Weekly copies are the lever while exports are full snapshots. A 20 GB export four times a month, eight copies retained, is about **$2.40** of R2 and about **$3.60** of Professional file-egress overage when the 50 GB allowance is otherwise free, against about **$66** for the daily schedule. The same archive, a different product. Convex publishes no incremental or diff export, only the full ZIP and a separate streaming path that is still a full read ([Data export](https://docs.convex.dev/database/import-export/export)). A labeled illustration in which 5 percent of bytes change per day would cut 30 days of stored full copies by about **12 times**. That change rate is not a Convex measurement, and a restore would still read about one full copy. No public source gives export throughput, so there is no evidenced size at which a job overlaps the next interval. The open-source one-export lock means a slow daily job is also a correctness problem, not only a bill.

Price the agency console at **$99 per month for up to 20 deployments**, with operator-held R2 included for deployments in the solo and typical band, overage when retained GB-month would push storage margin under 70 percent, and customer R2 as a first-class destination with no storage meter. Price a single Professional team that wants the operator to hold archives at **$15 per deployment per month**. Keep daily schedules for exports around 2 GB, where the customer’s Professional file-egress overage is a few dollars. Move exports in the tens of gigabytes to a weekly full copy. Point a solo developer with one deployment at the open-source binary on a machine they already run: a dedicated VPS per 200 MB deployment would dwarf **$0.04** of R2, and that developer is the user the self-hosted product is for. These margins exclude support. A $5 solo plan with $0.04 of storage fails a 70 percent margin as soon as a human spends more than about **$1.50** on that account.

## Tenancy, sessions, and a VPS wrapping key are the launch work

The repository today is a single-tenant install, and the product spec treats a hosted SaaS as a non-goal while already naming agencies with many client deployments as an audience ([product spec](https://github.com/KodyDennon/ConvexAutoBackup/blob/main/docs/PRODUCT_SPEC.md)). The live database is SQLite. Teams exist, and projects and destinations carry a team id, but users, tokens, secrets, targets, jobs, runs, schedules, and audit events do not. New projects are written to a single default team. List queries read whole tables. One install-wide key, `SHA-256` of `CONVEX_AUTOBACKUP_MASTER_KEY`, decrypts every secret with AES-256-GCM ([secrets](https://github.com/KodyDennon/ConvexAutoBackup/blob/main/crates/core/src/secrets.rs), [architecture](https://github.com/KodyDennon/ConvexAutoBackup/blob/main/docs/ARCHITECTURE.md)). That design is an honest self-hosted boundary: the operator holds the master key on their own machine. It is a cross-customer breach if two paying customers share the file. Adding a “hosted” flag on that schema is how one agency client reads another’s deploy key.

The early hosted shape is one Postgres database on the same VPS, shared tables, a `tenant_id` on every customer-owned row, and row-level security as a backstop. OWASP’s multi-tenant guidance treats the shared-table approach as viable when tenant ownership is enforced, the application role is not a superuser and does not have `BYPASSRLS`, tenant context is transaction-local, and a missing tenant fails closed ([OWASP multi-tenant cheat sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Multi_Tenant_Security_Cheat_Sheet.html)). The agency is one tenant. Each client Convex project is a project inside that tenant, with membership grants. A second paying customer is a different `tenant_id`. A viewer who can guess another client’s target id gets a 404. Postgres RLS using `tenant_id = current_setting('app.current_tenant')` is the belt behind the request’s `WHERE` clause. Schema-per-tenant and one process per customer are later isolation upgrades. One process per customer is today’s security boundary repeated N times, and it fights agency login across clients. The open-source build stays on SQLite with one team and one master key. Hosted mode is a second build of the same domain model. Retrofitting tenant ids after customer secrets and archives exist is a migration of production credentials, which is why this work comes before the first paying tenant.

Authentication that can take money is smaller than enterprise SSO and larger than what the server does now. Passwords are already Argon2 with a 12-character minimum. Login, though, verifies the password and inserts another API token named `web-login` with no expiry column, and the server accepts only `Authorization: Bearer` ([server login](https://github.com/KodyDennon/ConvexAutoBackup/blob/main/crates/server/src/lib.rs)). Every successful login leaves another live bearer credential. Roles are global to the install. Audit records for user and token creation pass the actor `"system"`. Before custody is sold, the hosted service needs verified email, password reset, login rate limits, and a browser session: an httpOnly, Secure, SameSite cookie, server-side expiry and revocation, and CSRF on cookie-authenticated mutations. API tokens need expiry, last-used time, and scopes, so a token that runs backups cannot restore. MFA, TOTP or WebAuthn, applies to anyone who can create tokens, unwrap a deploy key, or start a restore. OWASP ASVS 3.7.1 requires a full session, re-authentication, or a second factor before sensitive transactions ([OWASP ASVS session management](https://github.com/OWASP/ASVS/blob/master/4.0/en/0x12-V3-Session-management.md)). Magic-link login can replace passwords. Google or GitHub OAuth is fine for a solo operator and does nothing for tenancy. SAML, OIDC enterprise SSO, and SCIM wait until a buyer’s review says they are required, and they wait until membership exists so identities attach to a tenant. The self-hosted first-run owner bootstrap stays the open-source flow. Hosted signup is public registration tied to a billing identity, and the “bootstrap closes after the first user” rule is the wrong gate once the install is multi-tenant.

Secret encryption stays on the VPS. Envelope encryption, as cloud key services define it, means the application encrypts data with a unique data key and stores that data key only in wrapped form under a wrapping key; decrypt fails unless the same encryption context is supplied, and losing the wrapping key makes the data unrecoverable ([AWS KMS FAQ](https://aws.amazon.com/kms/faqs/), [AWS Encryption SDK concepts](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/encryption-sdk/latest/developer-guide/concepts.html)). Those documents describe the pattern. This product does not call AWS, and it does not need a key-management service to implement the pattern. Generate a 256-bit wrapping key on the VPS. Store it in a root-owned file on the encrypted disk, readable by the worker user, excluded from database dumps and from R2. For each tenant, generate a data key, wrap it with AES-256-GCM, and bind the tenant id as additional authenticated data so a wrapped key copied from tenant A’s row fails closed if presented as tenant B. Encrypt deploy keys, customer R2 credentials, and age passphrases with that data key. The worker unwraps only for the duration of that tenant’s export, passes `CONVEX_DEPLOY_KEY` to the child process the way it does today, and drops it when the process exits. NIST’s SC-12 and SC-28 are the control statements for managing keys and protecting data at rest, including protected storage for the key material ([NIST SC-12](https://csf.tools/reference/nist-sp-800-53/r5/sc/sc-12/), [NIST SC-28](https://csf.tools/reference/nist-sp-800-53/r5/sc/sc-28/)). On a single VPS the operator administers, this is weaker than a hardware-backed key the application cannot export: the worker process can unwrap, and anyone who can read both the database and the wrapping-key file can decrypt every deploy key. The security page says that in one sentence. Custody is the product. An age passphrase stored in the same vault does not create a second party. A customer who wants archives the operator cannot read holds the age identity outside the service; unattended backup in that mode is a later product, because scheduled export then needs a grant the operator does not have.

Customer R2 removes archive custody and leaves deploy-key custody in place. The export still needs the plaintext key in the worker. The destination’s credentials are a secret of that tenant. The bucket policy allows that key to `PutObject` and to complete multipart uploads under a tenant prefix, and the customer denies `GetObject` and `ListBucket` to the operator. Ship a test that the control-plane credentials cannot read the object. Object keys in the operator’s own bucket are prefixed by tenant id. Restore selects a deployment record in the same tenant as the archive. The existing typed deployment name and safety phrase stay; they stop a fat-finger, and they do not authorize a cross-tenant write ([restore](https://github.com/KodyDennon/ConvexAutoBackup/blob/main/crates/core/src/restore.rs)). Delete-tenant revokes tokens, destroys or returns secrets, and deletes archives the operator can still reach. If the customer bucket denies delete, the contract says the customer deletes. Operator staff who need access use a break-glass role that is audited and is absent from the customer’s user table. Three failures end the company if they happen: theft of deploy keys, a read or restore that crosses tenants, and a backup that cannot be restored. A checksum without a customer-visible drill covers only the third on paper. GDPR Article 32 names the ability to restore, and regular testing of that ability, as part of security of processing once the archives contain personal data ([GDPR Article 32](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-32-gdpr/)).

## A DPA starts the sale, and SOC 2 waits on procurement buyers

The first ten design partners can be sold with no SOC 2 report. They are developers and small agencies whose security conversation is a call plus a page. The page states that the operator holds deploy keys, that the operator can decrypt, where archives live, which subprocessors and regions are in the path, how a tenant is deleted, and how restore is tested. A one-page data-processing agreement and a subprocessor list are that page in contract form. A trust-center banner that says SOC 2 is in progress leaves the actual controls undescribed. Convex Professional already includes Convex’s own HIPAA and SOC 2 reports ([Convex pricing](https://www.convex.dev/pricing)). Customers who need Convex’s report can have it and still lack a customer-held archive. Customers who need the backup vendor’s report are a different sale.

That different sale shows up as a questionnaire before it shows up as a logo requirement. SIG Lite is **128 questions**, aimed at SMB and mid-market reviews and estimated around **48 hours** of subject-matter time; SIG Core is **627 questions** for vendors handling sensitive data ([Evident on SIG 2025](https://trustmind.com/blog/sig-vs-sig-lite-vs-sig-core/)). This product is a poor fit for a “low-risk vendor” box, because the vendor holds production archives and a key that can export the live database. Expect a short custom form from a 30-person company that has any vendor review, and SIG Core once a security team classifies the backup host as critical. SOC 2 Type II is the artifact that substitutes for answering those questionnaires. It becomes the right project when the founder decides to sell to buyers whose checklist says the report or no vendor. Type I opines on design at a point in time. Type II tests operating effectiveness over a period. The AICPA guide does not set a universal minimum length for that period ([AICPA SOC 2 guide excerpt](https://assets.ctfassets.net/rb9cdnjh59cm/2paxzm46AxmwDDSZGkdtFS/c01813c67d26de154898653c121a92ea/AAGSOP22E.pdf)). Practitioners treat three months as a usual floor, six months as a common first Type II, and twelve months as what many enterprise buyers eventually expect, and they treat the three-month figure as convention the engaged CPA must accept ([soc2auditors.org observation period](https://soc2auditors.org/insights/soc-2-observation-period-explained/)).

Cash, when that day comes, is a real budget and a noisy one. A directory updated **5 October 2026** puts the median Type II quote ballpark for a 1–10 person team at **$9,000**, and its planning table for a prepared Security-only team of that size at **$10,000 to $30,500** cash in year one, plus staff time valued at **$25,000 to $90,000** that is not in the cash total ([soc2auditors.org audit cost](https://soc2auditors.org/soc-2-audit-cost/)). A 2026 founder writeup puts small-SaaS Type II year one at **$25,000 to $50,000** all-in ([Patotski, SOC 2 cost, 2026](https://patotski.com/blog/soc-2-cost-for-startup-saas/)). Vanta, which sells the automation tool, publishes **$20,000 to $80,000+** and claims about **98 percent** of Fortune 500 buyers require a Type II report, without a method on the page ([Vanta, SOC 2 for startups](https://www.vanta.com/collection/soc-2/soc-2-for-startups)). A consultancy page that adds Availability, Confidentiality, and a large fraction of a full-time employee reaches **$50,000 to $120,000** ([YuSMP, 2026](https://yusmpgroup.com/blog/soc2-type2-saas-startups)). The figure to plan on, for a tiny team that has already built the controls and buys a Security-only examination, is on the order of **$10,000 to $30,000** cash in year one and a similar amount in year two. The higher bands are what a platform subscription and a wider criteria set cost. The founder’s rule is the right one: build the controls in the product, then pay a CPA to test them. A compliance subscription that collects policy screenshots does not create tenant isolation, and an observation window started on today’s single master key produces a report about a system that must then be replaced. Year two does not go to zero.

GDPR applies as soon as an EU customer’s production database, which is personal data, sits in archives on a US VPS, whether or not anyone has asked for SOC 2. The customer is the controller. The hosted operator is the processor. Article 28 requires a contract that states the subject matter, duration, nature, purpose, data types, and data-subject categories, and it requires confidentiality, security, subprocessor terms, assistance, deletion or return, and audits ([GDPR Article 28](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/28)). The practical minimum before accepting those archives is the DPA, the subprocessor list (VPS host, R2, email, error tracking), encryption in transit and at rest, a retention job that actually deletes objects, a way to export or delete a tenant, and a transfer tool. EU Standard Contractual Clauses are that tool, and their storage-limitation text requires erasure or anonymisation of the data and of backups at the end of the retention period ([European Commission, SCCs](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/international-dimension-data-protection/standard-contractual-clauses-scc_en)). The EU-US Data Privacy Framework adequacy decision was still in force as of early October 2026, with an appeal pending and with commentators treating the June 2026 US Supreme Court decision on removal of FTC commissioners as a live threat to it ([Skadden, July 2026](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/07/supreme-court-decision-raises-new-questions), [Lindstead, 1 October 2026](https://lindstead.com/guides/schrems-ii-ai)). Use the clauses. Customer-owned R2 in the EU shrinks the archive transfer. The deploy key still sits in the US control plane if the operator does. Whether Convex’s own customer contract forbids a third party from storing deploy keys was not checked. That reading belongs in the launch checklist beside the DPA.

## Conclusion

Customer R2 and operator-held R2 are one product with two answers to the same cliff. Operator storage stays cheap through a 2 GB daily archive, so those clients can live in the operator’s bucket inside a **$99** workspace. A 20 GB daily archive still shows a healthy storage margin at **$15** and still costs the customer about **$70** at Convex, so that client belongs on a weekly schedule or in a bucket the client pays for. The open-source binary remains the path for the solo developer who will hold the master key themselves. The SOC 2 clock starts only after Postgres tenancy, sessions, MFA, and a VPS wrapping key kept out of database dumps are the system a CPA would be watching, and only once the founder has chosen buyers whose checklist requires the report. An examination bought against today’s single install-wide key would describe a design the first paying tenant has to leave behind.
